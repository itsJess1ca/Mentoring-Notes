## Panel Interviews vs. One-on-One

### Preparing for Panel Interviews
- Research each panel member beforehand - know their roles and how they might evaluate you
- Prepare for different question styles - the technical lead will ask different things than the product manager
- Practice maintaining energy with multiple people - it's more draining than one-on-one

### Managing Multiple Interviewers
- Address the person who asked the question, but make eye contact with others during your answer
- Don't just focus on the most senior person - everyone's opinion matters
- If someone looks disengaged, try to bring them in: "I'd be curious to hear your perspective on this"

### Eye Contact and Engagement Strategies
- Start your answer looking at the questioner, then scan to include others
- If it's a round table, imagine you're presenting to the whole room, not just one person
- Watch for non-verbal cues - if someone looks confused, pause and ask if they'd like clarification

### One-on-One Interview Advantages
- Easier to build rapport and have a natural conversation
- You can adapt your communication style to match theirs
- More opportunity for follow-up questions and deeper discussion

## Video Interview Best Practices

### Technical Setup and Testing
- Test your setup 30 minutes before - not 5 minutes. I've seen too many people scramble with audio issues
- Have a backup plan: phone number ready, different browser, mobile hotspot if your wifi is unreliable
- Close everything else on your computer - you don't want notifications popping up during the interview

### Professional Appearance and Environment
- Dress as you would for an in-person interview - it affects your mindset too
- Check your background - plain wall or a bookshelf is fine, but avoid anything distracting
- Good lighting is crucial - face a window if possible, or get a cheap ring light

### Managing Technical Difficulties
- If something goes wrong, stay calm and communicate: "I'm having audio issues, let me try reconnecting"
- Have the interviewer's phone number as backup - don't waste 10 minutes trying to fix video
- Most interviewers are understanding about tech issues, but don't let it fluster you

### Body Language and Engagement on Camera
- Look at the camera, not the screen - it's hard at first but makes a huge difference for eye contact
- Sit up straight and gesture normally - don't be stiff because it's on camera
- Nod and react visually more than you normally would - video flattens expressions

## Take-Home Assignments and Time Management

### Understanding Assignment Expectations
- Ask clarifying questions upfront: "Are you looking for production-ready code or a working prototype?"
- Understand the time expectation - if they say "a few hours" but you spent 8, that's a mismatch
- Clarify if you should focus on functionality, code quality, or demonstrating specific skills

### Time Management Strategies
- Set a hard limit for yourself - I usually cap take-homes at 4 hours regardless of what they say
- Start with the core functionality, then add polish if you have time
- Track your time and be honest about it in your submission - "This took me about 3 hours focused time"

### Quality vs. Scope Balance
- Better to do fewer things well than many things poorly
- Include a README explaining what you'd do next with more time
- Show your thinking process - comments, commit messages, and documentation matter

### Following Up and Presenting Your Work
- Include instructions for running your code - don't make them guess
- Be prepared to walk through your solution and explain your decisions
- Talk about trade-offs you made and what you'd do differently in a production environment
