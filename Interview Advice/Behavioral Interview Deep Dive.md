## Behavioural Interview Methods

For detailed guidance on structured response methods, see:
- [[STAR Method]] - The classic Situation-Task-Action-Result framework
- [[CAR Method]] - Challenge-Action-Result approach for problem-focused questions
- [[STAR vs CAR Comparison]] - When to use each method

### Advanced Techniques Beyond Basic Frameworks
- **The Bridge Technique**: Connect your story to the role: "This experience taught me the importance of thorough testing, which I know is crucial for this position"
- **The Growth Narrative**: Show progression: "Early in my career I would have just fixed the bug, but now I think about root causes and prevention"
- **The Team Impact Focus**: Even for individual achievements, mention team benefits: "My solution not only solved the immediate problem but also created a reusable component for the team"
- **The Learning Highlight**: Always include what you learned: "This taught me to involve stakeholders earlier in the design process"
- **The Future Application**: Show how you'd apply lessons: "Now I always build in buffer time for testing based on that experience"

### Adapting Methods to Different Question Types
- **Leadership questions**: Use STAR to show progression from individual contributor to influencing others
- **Technical problem-solving**: CAR works well to emphasize the complexity of the challenge
- **Failure questions**: Use CAR but spend extra time on the Result and what you learned
- **Teamwork questions**: STAR helps distinguish your individual contribution within team success
- **Initiative questions**: CAR emphasizes the challenge you identified and chose to tackle
- **Learning questions**: Either method works, but focus on the process of acquiring new skills

### Building a Story Bank for Multiple Methods
- Prepare 5-7 core stories that can be adapted for different questions
- Practice telling the same story with both STAR and CAR to see which feels more natural
- Map each story to specific skills: technical problem-solving, leadership, collaboration, learning agility
- Use your [[Brag Doc]] to identify stories that show progression over your career
- Include both individual achievements and team contributions
- Prepare stories from different contexts: work projects, side projects, learning experiences
- Have a mix of technical and soft skill stories ready

## Preparing Stories for Common Behavioural Questions

### Leadership and Initiative Questions
- **Common questions**: "Tell me about a time you led a project", "Describe when you took initiative", "How have you influenced without authority?"
- **For mid-level developers**: Focus on technical leadership, mentoring newer developers, or improving processes
- **Example angle**: Leading a refactoring effort, organizing team learning sessions, or proposing technical solutions
- **Show progression**: "As I've grown more confident, I've started speaking up more in architectural discussions"
- **Emphasize collaboration**: Even when leading, show how you brought others along
- **Include measurement**: How did your leadership impact the team or project outcomes?

### Teamwork and Collaboration Questions
- **Common questions**: "Describe your ideal team environment", "Tell me about a difficult team situation", "How do you handle disagreements?"
- **For mid-level developers**: Show you can work independently but also support others
- **Example situations**: Code review disagreements, helping junior developers, cross-team collaboration
- **Show emotional intelligence**: Understanding different perspectives and communication styles
- **Emphasize growth**: How you've learned to give better feedback or communicate more effectively
- **Include outcomes**: How did your collaboration improve the team or project?
- **Show adaptability**: Working with different personality types or working styles

### Problem-Solving and Conflict Resolution Questions
- **Common questions**: "Tell me about a complex technical problem you solved", "Describe a time you disagreed with a teammate", "How do you handle competing priorities?"
- **For mid-level developers**: Show systematic problem-solving and professional conflict resolution
- **Technical examples**: Debugging complex issues, performance optimization, architecture decisions
- **Interpersonal examples**: Code review feedback, different technical approaches, priority disagreements
- **Show methodology**: How you break down problems, gather information, and evaluate solutions
- **Emphasize communication**: How you explained technical concepts or facilitated resolution
- **Include prevention**: What you learned to avoid similar issues in the future

### Adaptability and Learning Questions
- **Common questions**: "Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly", "How do you stay current with technology?", "Describe a time requirements changed mid-project"
- **For mid-level developers**: Show continuous learning and ability to handle changing requirements
- **Learning examples**: Picking up new frameworks, understanding legacy codebases, learning domain knowledge
- **Adaptability examples**: Scope changes, priority shifts, new team members, technology pivots
- **Show learning process**: How you approach unfamiliar technologies or concepts
- **Emphasize practical application**: How you applied new knowledge to solve real problems
- **Include teaching**: How you shared new knowledge with teammates

## Handling "Weakness" and "Failure" Questions

### Choosing Appropriate Weaknesses to Share
- **Pick real weaknesses that don't disqualify you**: "I sometimes focus too much on perfecting code instead of shipping iteratively"
- **Show self-awareness**: Demonstrate you understand the impact of this weakness
- **Choose growth areas relevant to your level**: "I'm still learning to balance technical debt with feature delivery"
- **Avoid fake weaknesses**: Don't say "I work too hard" or "I'm a perfectionist" unless you can make it genuine
- **Pick something you're actively working on**: "I've been working on this by..."
- **For mid-level developers**: Focus on areas like technical communication, estimation, or balancing different priorities

### Framing Failures as Learning Opportunities
- **Be genuinely honest**: Pick a real failure, not something that was actually a success
- **Take responsibility**: Don't blame others, even if they contributed to the failure
- **Show the impact**: Acknowledge what went wrong and why it mattered
- **Focus on learning**: What specific lessons did you take away?
- **Demonstrate change**: How do you do things differently now?
- **For mid-level developers**: Good failures include estimation errors, technical decisions that didn't work out, or communication breakdowns
- **Show resilience**: How you bounced back and regained team confidence

### Demonstrating Growth and Self-Awareness
- **Show progression over time**: "Early in my career I would..., but now I..."
- **Reference feedback you've received**: "My manager pointed out that I needed to..., so I worked on..."
- **Acknowledge ongoing development**: "I'm still working on this, but I've made progress by..."
- **Use specific examples**: Don't just say you've grown, show how with concrete situations
- **Connect to the role**: How does your self-awareness make you a better fit for this position?
- **For mid-level developers**: Show awareness of your current capabilities and next growth areas
- **Include seeking feedback**: How you actively work to understand your blind spots

### Avoiding Common Pitfalls
- **Don't choose weaknesses that are actually strengths**: "I care too much about quality" sounds insincere
- **Don't pick deal-breakers**: Don't mention weaknesses that would disqualify you from the role. 
	- [Some good examples of good answers to this question](https://dovetail.com/employee-experience/good-weaknesses-for-job-interviews/#17-potential-answers-when-asked-about-your-weaknesses-in-an-interview)
- **Don't be vague**: "I need to improve my communication" isn't specific enough
- **Don't stop at the weakness**: Always include what you're doing about it
- **Don't pick the same weakness as everyone else**: "Public speaking" is overused
- **Don't sound rehearsed**: Practice, but make sure it sounds natural and genuine
- **Don't minimize the failure**: If you're sharing a failure, own it completely
