## Follow-up Etiquette

### Thank You Email Templates and Timing
- Send within 24 hours - same day is even better if it was a morning interview
- Keep it brief but personal: "Thanks for taking the time to chat about the [specific role]. I'm particularly excited about [specific thing they mentioned] and think my experience with [relevant skill] would be valuable."
- Reference something specific from the conversation - shows you were engaged

### How to Follow Up Without Being Pushy
- Wait for their stated timeline plus 2-3 business days before following up
- One follow-up is professional, three is pushy. If they don't respond after two attempts, move on
- Frame it as checking in, not demanding an answer: "I wanted to check in on the timeline for next steps"

### Managing Multiple Interview Processes
- Keep a spreadsheet - company, role, stage, timeline, contacts. You'll forget details otherwise
- Be honest about timelines if asked: "I'm in final rounds with another company and should hear back by Friday"
- Don't use fake competing offers to speed up a process - it often backfires

### What to Do When You Don't Hear Back
- Assume good intent - they're probably just busy, not deliberately ignoring you
- One polite follow-up after their stated timeline is fine
- After that, assume it's a no and keep moving. Sometimes companies have budget freezes or internal changes

## Offer Evaluation

### Beyond Salary: Benefits, Equity, Career Growth
- Calculate total compensation, not just base salary - health insurance, retirement matching, equity can add up significantly
- Ask about growth opportunities: "What does career progression look like for someone in this role?"
- Consider work-life balance factors: commute, flexible hours, remote work options

### Evaluating Company Culture and Fit
- Trust your gut about the people you met - you'll be working with them daily
- Ask yourself: "Did they seem excited about their work?" and "Do I feel energized or drained after talking to them?"
- Pay attention to how they talk about challenges - do they seem collaborative or blame-focused?

### Understanding Total Compensation Packages
- Break down equity - how much, what type, vesting schedule, what's it potentially worth?
- Factor in benefits cost - good health insurance can be worth thousands per year
- Consider professional development budget, conference attendance, equipment allowances

### Red Flags to Watch For in Offers
- Pressure to decide immediately - legitimate offers usually give you at least a few days
- Vague job description or responsibilities - "you'll wear many hats" can mean unclear expectations
- Equity with no clear vesting schedule or explanation of company valuation

## Negotiation Tactics and Timing

### When and How to Negotiate
- Almost everything is negotiable - salary, start date, equipment, professional development budget
- Do your research first - know market rates for your role and location
- Frame it positively: "I'm excited about the role. Based on my research and experience, I was hoping we could discuss the compensation package"

### Negotiating Non-Salary Benefits
- If they can't move on salary, ask about signing bonus, extra vacation days, or flexible start date
- Professional development budget is often easier for companies to approve than salary increases
- Equipment upgrades (better laptop, monitor setup) are usually small costs for companies

### Handling Multiple Offers
- Don't pit companies against each other unless you're genuinely interested in both
- Be transparent about timelines: "I have another offer with a deadline of Friday, but I'm very interested in this role"
- Consider which role sets you up better for your next career move, not just immediate compensation

### Professional Ways to Decline Offers
- Be gracious and specific: "After careful consideration, I've decided to accept another offer that's a better fit for my career goals"
- Don't burn bridges - the industry is smaller than you think
- Offer to keep in touch or refer other candidates if appropriate
